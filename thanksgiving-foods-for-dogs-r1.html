Quick Answer:

Some Thanksgiving ingredients can be shared in small amounts if they fit your dog’s diet: fully cooked, skinless, boneless, unseasoned turkey, plain cooked sweet potato, plain pumpkin, and plain green beans. Ask your veterinarian before offering a new food, and keep bones, fatty or seasoned dishes, and desserts out of reach. If your dog eats a potentially toxic food, call your veterinarian or pet poison control immediately; suspected xylitol ingestion requires immediate veterinary care.[1][9][16][17]

📋 Key Takeaways

  • For turkey specifically, set aside skinless, boneless meat before seasoning and cook it fully without added ingredients. Offer only a small amount if it fits your dog’s diet; skip seasoned meat, skin, bones, and pan drippings.[1][8]
  • Skip stuffing, gravy, casseroles, pies, and desserts: recipes vary, and they may contain excess fat, onion, garlic, raisins, chocolate, or xylitol.[10][11][16]
  • If your dog eats chocolate, grapes, raisins, alcohol, or raw yeast dough, call your veterinarian or pet poison control immediately, without waiting for symptoms. For suspected xylitol ingestion, seek veterinary care immediately.[12][16][17]

Thanksgiving is built around sharing food, and most dogs will spend the day within sniffing distance of the table. The good news is that some plain, properly prepared Thanksgiving foods can be shared in small amounts. The risk sits in the preparation around them: butter, salt, onions, garlic, bones, and desserts. This guide to Thanksgiving foods for dogs separates plain ingredients from finished dishes, explains which Thanksgiving foods dogs should avoid, and covers what to do if your dog eats something harmful.

Quick Guide to Thanksgiving Foods for Dogs

The table below compares plain ingredients with the dishes that usually reach the table; it covers the foods discussed in this guide rather than every dish at the meal. “Plain” means cooked without salt, butter, oil, sauce, seasoning, onion, or garlic. Suitability still depends on your dog’s size, health, and any veterinary diet, so treat this as a starting point rather than a permission slip. Ask your veterinarian before offering a new food, especially if your dog has a medical condition, food sensitivities, or a prescribed diet. Keep all treats and extras combined below 10% of your dog’s daily calorie intake, and ask your veterinary team to set your dog’s individual allowance.[8][9]

Which Thanksgiving Foods Can Dogs Eat?

Plain ingredients may be an option in small amounts if they suit your dog’s diet; check with your veterinarian before adding a new food. A food that can be offered plain may become unsuitable once it is baked into a casserole or finished with gravy. Judge the plate by what was added, not by the main ingredient.[8][9][10]

Can Dogs Eat Turkey?

Plain, fully cooked, skinless, boneless, unseasoned white turkey meat may be offered in small amounts if it fits your dog’s diet. Ask your veterinarian before offering it for the first time. Skip turkey prepared with butter, salt, onion, garlic, gravy, or stuffing. Skin and fatty scraps can cause digestive upset and pancreatitis. Set aside a plain portion before seasoning, then cook it fully before serving.[1][9][11][14]

Can Dogs Eat Sweet Potatoes?

Plain cooked sweet potato can be offered in small amounts if it fits your dog’s diet; ask your veterinarian before offering it for the first time. Avoid versions made with added ingredients, such as sweet potato casserole with butter, brown sugar, marshmallows, and spice. Those additions turn a simple vegetable into a dessert. Serve it plain, mashed or cubed, with no salt or syrup, and keep the portion small enough that it does not replace a meal.[8][9][10] For a related recipe article, see our guide to homemade sweet potato dog treats.

Can Dogs Eat Green Beans?

If they fit your dog’s diet, plain green beans may be offered in small amounts: steamed or boiled, unsalted, unbuttered, and cut into bite-size pieces. Green bean casserole is not the same food. Recipes may contain cream of mushroom soup, fried onions, butter, and salt, and the onion content alone is a concern because onions and garlic can damage a dog’s red blood cells. If your dog has a history of pancreatitis, food sensitivities, or a prescribed diet, ask your veterinary team what treats are appropriate before offering any.[8][10][14]

Can Dogs Eat Pumpkin?

If it fits your dog’s diet, plain pumpkin may be offered in small amounts: canned pumpkin with one ingredient, or pumpkin you cooked and mashed yourself. Pumpkin pie filling is a different product, and recipes vary. Choose pure pumpkin rather than sweetened or spiced pie filling. Read the ingredient list rather than the front of the can. Some sugar-free or “no sugar added” products may be sweetened with xylitol, which can cause low blood sugar and liver injury in dogs and is a medical emergency rather than a stomach upset.[5] Check with your veterinarian before introducing pumpkin. If you suspect your dog has eaten xylitol, seek veterinary care immediately. Skip anything you cannot verify.[9][10][16]

Thanksgiving Foods Dogs Should Avoid

Thanksgiving hazards include both plain ingredients and prepared dishes. Keep bones, fatty scraps, foods containing onion or garlic, and potentially toxic foods out of reach. The groups below cover common holiday hazards, not every possible exposure.[11][14][17]

Turkey Bones, Skin, Fat, and Gravy

Cooked poultry bones are a serious hazard. They can splinter into sharp pieces that may cause choking, internal injuries, or a blockage.[1][11] Do not give your dog raw or cooked turkey bones.[8] Skin and pan drippings are a separate problem: high-fat foods can cause digestive upset and pancreatitis, and gravy may contain onion or garlic. Keep the carcass, carving board, and roasting pan out of reach. Put bones and fatty scraps in a secured trash bin as you clear them away.[11][14]

Stuffing, Casseroles, Onions, and Garlic

Stuffing and casseroles are combination dishes, and that is exactly why they are risky. Stuffing may contain onion, garlic, butter, salt, raisins, or sausage; casseroles may include cream, fried onions, sugar, or marshmallows. Onions, garlic, leeks, and chives belong to the allium family, which can damage red blood cells and cause anemia in dogs, and the ASPCA lists them among foods to avoid feeding pets [2]. If your dog eats a dish containing onion or garlic, contact your veterinarian or pet poison control promptly; signs of poisoning may be delayed. For a vegetable your veterinarian has confirmed is appropriate for your dog, set aside a plain portion before the casserole is assembled.[9][14][17]

Pies, Chocolate, Raisins, and Xylitol

Desserts are not one risk but several, which is why the answer depends on the recipe. Chocolate contains the methylxanthines theobromine and caffeine; risk depends on the type and amount eaten and the dog’s weight [3]. Grapes and raisins can cause kidney injury in dogs; if your dog eats either, contact your veterinarian or pet poison control immediately [4]. Xylitol, a sugar substitute found in some sugar-free pies, candies, gum, and peanut butter, can cause a rapid insulin release and liver damage [5][16]. If your dog may have eaten xylitol, seek veterinary care immediately.[16] A fruit pie may also contain raisins; a “sugar-free” pie may contain xylitol. Ask what is in the dessert before you assume, and keep desserts and unattended plates out of your dog’s reach.[18]

Alcohol, Yeast Dough, and Other Dangerous Leftovers

Alcoholic drinks and foods containing alcohol can cause vomiting, diarrhea, loss of coordination, tremors, and difficulty breathing in dogs.[2] Raw yeast dough can expand in the stomach and produce alcohol; it can cause painful bloating and potentially stomach twisting, a life-threatening emergency.[2] If your dog consumes alcohol or raw yeast dough, contact your veterinarian or pet poison control immediately.[17] Other leftovers to lock away include coffee grounds, macadamia nuts, moldy food, and any dish containing garlic or onion.[14][15][17][19]

What If Your Dog Eats Something Unsafe?

Stay calm, but do not wait for symptoms. If your dog is unconscious, having trouble breathing, bleeding, or having seizures, go directly to an emergency veterinary clinic. Suspected xylitol ingestion also requires immediate veterinary care. Otherwise, prevent further access and call your veterinarian or pet poison control immediately. Gather the details below without delaying that call.[12][16]

  1. Prevent further access: move the plate, close the trash, and keep your dog where you can observe them while arranging help.[12]
  2. While arranging help, note the food, ingredients, approximate amount, and time of ingestion. Have the packaging or a photo ready if available, but do not delay the call or urgent veterinary care to collect it.[12]
  3. Keep the packaging or ingredient list to help the veterinary team identify what and how much your dog ate.[12]
  4. Contact your veterinarian or pet poison control immediately for suspected poisoning. If your dog swallowed a bone, contact your veterinarian promptly. Report vomiting, diarrhea, tremors, lethargy, or other changes right away and follow the veterinary team’s instructions. In the U.S., ASPCA Poison Control is available at (888) 426-4435 and Pet Poison Helpline at (855) 764-7661, both 24/7. A consultation fee may apply for ASPCA Poison Control; Pet Poison Helpline charges an incident fee.[6][7][11][12]
  5. Do not induce vomiting or give home remedies unless a veterinary professional instructs you to do so.[7]

The absence of immediate symptoms does not mean your dog is safe. Some signs of poisoning may take hours or days to appear. Contact a veterinary professional immediately after a suspected toxic ingestion, then keep observing and follow the advice you are given.[12][14][16]

How to Keep Your Dog Safe During Thanksgiving Dinner

Plan ahead to keep food, leftovers, and trash out of reach, and ask guests not to feed your dog from their plates.[18]

  • Brief your guests before the meal. Ask them not to feed your dog from the table.[18]
  • Set up a quiet space away from the table with water and a familiar bed. If you offer a chew, stay with your dog and supervise throughout.[13][18]
  • Keep trash and compost bins secured. Dispose of bones and fatty scraps promptly in a bin your dog cannot access.[11][19]
  • If your veterinarian agrees that a food fits your dog’s diet, set aside a plain portion before seasoning; cook turkey fully before serving.[1][9]
  • Keep your dog’s usual feeding routine. If your dog has a medical condition, food sensitivities, or a prescribed diet, ask your veterinary team before offering holiday foods or extra treats.[8][18]

If your veterinarian agrees that a holiday food is appropriate for your dog, offer only a small, plain portion within the daily treat allowance. Stop offering a new treat if your dog becomes unwell and contact your veterinarian promptly.[8][9][11]

Frequently Asked Questions

Can dogs eat turkey on Thanksgiving?

Yes, if it fits your dog’s diet and your veterinarian agrees. Offer only a small amount of fully cooked, skinless, boneless, unseasoned white turkey meat. Skip the skin, drippings, gravy, and any meat prepared with butter, salt, garlic, or onion. Do not feed raw or cooked turkey bones.[1][8][9]

Are turkey bones safe for dogs?

No. Cooked turkey bones can splinter and cause choking, internal injuries, or a blockage. Do not treat the wishbone as a toy, and do not feed raw or cooked turkey bones.[1][8][11]

Can dogs eat stuffing?

Do not offer stuffing. Recipes may contain onion, garlic, butter, salt, or raisins. If your dog has eaten stuffing, check the ingredients and contact your veterinarian or pet poison control promptly if it contains a potentially harmful ingredient or you are unsure.[10][14][17]

What should I do if my dog eats something toxic?

If your dog is unconscious, having trouble breathing, bleeding, or having seizures, go directly to an emergency veterinary clinic. Suspected xylitol ingestion also requires immediate veterinary care. Otherwise, prevent further access and call your veterinarian or pet poison control immediately, even if your dog seems normal. Keep the packaging and note what was eaten, how much, and when, without delaying help. In the U.S., call ASPCA Poison Control at (888) 426-4435 or Pet Poison Helpline at (855) 764-7661, both available 24/7. Do not induce vomiting unless a veterinary professional instructs you to do so.[6][7][12][16]
